# How to Split Equity with Cofounders: The "Slicing Pie" Method

## Overview: The Core Thesis
In this presentation at Stanford, Mike Moyer outlines the core problem with traditional startup equity splits: **fixed equity splits are fundamentally flawed.** 

Most founders negotiate a fixed split (e.g., 50/50, 60/40) on day one, assuming they can predict the future value of the company and the future contributions of each founder. Because startups are inherently volatile and people's life circumstances change (someone quits, someone stops working hard, the strategy pivots), a fixed split inevitably leaves someone with more than their fair share, creating resentment. 

The solution is a **Dynamic Equity Split** (the "Slicing Pie" model). In this model, equity is continuously allocated based on the actual, quantifiable value of the contributions (time, cash, ideas, relationships) that each participant makes over time, until the company can pay actual cash.

## Key Concepts & Challenges

### 1. The Value Formula (Theoretical Value)
Because a startup is fundamentally worth $0 at the beginning, you cannot base equity on theoretical company valuation. Instead, you calculate equity based on a **Theoretical Base Value (TBV)** of the inputs.
- **Your Share % =** (Theoretical Value of Your Contributions) / (Total Theoretical Value of Everyone's Contributions).
- You keep tracking this until the company starts generating enough cash to pay people their market rates, at which point the equity pie "bakes" and the percentages lock in.

### 2. Valuing Contributions (The Grunt Fund)
To make the math work, you apply specific multipliers to different types of risk:
- **Time (Sweat Equity):** Calculated using your Fair Market Salary (what you would get paid at a normal job). 
  - *Formula:* (Negotiated Base Salary - Actual Cash Paid) × 2 ÷ 2000 hours. 
  - The `× 2` multiplier accounts for the high risk of not getting paid at a startup.
- **Cash:** Cash is harder to get and save than time. 
  - *Formula:* Cash contributed × 4 (applied as it is actually *spent* by the company, not when deposited).
- **Ideas / Intellectual Property:** Valued using standard industry royalty rates (e.g., 5% of revenue), applied only if the idea actually generates business (meaning bad ideas/pivots don't accidentally earn a founder 50% of the company).
- **Relationships / Sales:** Treated like standard commission models, multiplied by the risk factor if unpaid.

### 3. Handling Exits & Separations
A critical part of the Slicing Pie model is how it handles someone leaving the company, based on whether the departure is fair or unfair to the startup:

*   **Fired for Good Reason (Performance/Conduct) OR Resigns for No Good Reason (Got a better job, quit):**
    *   *Consequence:* The individual loses their time-based equity. The company does not owe them for sweat equity, because quitting/underperforming hurts the startup. 
    *   Cash contributions revert to a 1x multiplier (the startup must try to pay it back, but the 4x premium is lost).
*   **Fired for No Good Reason (Laid off, pivot) OR Resigns for Good Reason (Company forced relocation, slashed salary):**
    *   *Consequence:* The individual keeps their equity at the full theoretical value (with multipliers). The company must respect the risk the person took, as the separation was the company's fault.

### 4. Avoiding the "Alligator Pit"
Traditional renegotiations of equity when a founder leaves or a new one joins are "alligator pits"—driven by self-preservation, fear, and greed. A dynamic split eliminates these negotiations because the math automatically adjusts. If a new developer works 40 hours, their theoretical value is added to the denominator, slightly diluting everyone else in a perfectly fair mathematical proportion.

---
*Note: This model completely replaces traditional 4-year vesting schedules with a cliff, as the protection mechanisms for founder departure are mathematically built into the contribution tracking.*
